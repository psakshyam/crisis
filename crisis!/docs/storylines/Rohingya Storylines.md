|  Explanation of Crisis  | Finalized Summary: Many of the world’s humanitarian crises revolve around the dynamics between religious and ideological majorities and their accompanying minorities. These dynamics are nothing new, and still persist. The Rohingya Muslim Crisis is an example of gross human rights abuses that still exist following a familiar structure. Rohingya Muslims in Myanmar are outnumbered by a Buddhist majority. Targeted, structural and official forms of discrimination against the minority has caused great displacement and internal chaos. The Rohingya struggle under this structurally entrenched otherization and entrapment within the civilization-savagism paradigm. Nassir Uddin’s *The Rohingya: An Ethnography of 'Subhuman' Life* outlines different methods in which the structural majority classified Rohingya muslims as subhuman, savages and lower forms of life. This labelling aligns with the ideas of Susan Benesch’s *Dangerous Speech Guide:* dehumanizing language and rhetoric is used to legitimize and necessitate violence against perceived threats. While forms of structural and rhetorical violence against the Rohingya Muslims has existed for decades, tensions flared after a 2017 operation to increase “stability” across Myanmar. Spun by the Myanmar government as a military offensive targeting only Rohingya militant groups, the United Nations views the operation as a genocidal endeavour.  An integral part of the government’s targeting boils down to the refusal to recognize the Rohingya as culturally burmese, nor recognize them as formal citizens. Not only have many Rohingya struggled due to disadvantaged material conditions, but the struggle to prove their own citizenship and belonging. Among many other major exclusions, the Rohingya were excluded from the 2014 census and not recognized as people. The Rohingya are plainly, a stateless people. Rohingya living within Myanmar are forced to carry identification cards which designate the group as non-citizen foreigners. Labelled illegal migrants from Bangledash, Bangledash ended up being a primary location for Rohingya’s to flee their government’s grasp. While there are an estimated 3.5 million Rohingya dispersed worldwide, the center of the displacement is in Bangladesh, where the refugee population has swelled to almost 1.2 million people as of early 2026\. Rohingya require permission to marry, and are legally forced to comply with expression laws in direct contrast with their muslim faith. As part of the 2017 operation, 288 individual Rohingya villages were eliminated, and approximately 7000 people perished in the very first month of attacks. Myanmar’s government capitalized upon the attacks of Arakan Rohingya Salvation Army against police and military sites, and the ARSA attacks were largely used as justification.  Over half a million children are growing up in the world’s largest refugee settlement in Cox’s Bazar, where more than 235,000 children remain out of school and over 60% of the water supply has been found contaminated. Still, approximately 600,000 Rohingya remain in Myanmar’s Rakhine State, where they continue to face a high risk of genocide and severe restrictions on their basic human rights Basic Facts: Timeline: August 2017 \- Present Place: Myanmar and Bangladesh   Status Quo: “ The government of Myanmar has launched an offensive targeting the Rohingya Muslim religious minority.”   UNHCR Report- Situation of Human Rights of Rohyingya Muslims and other minorities in Myanmar Over the four years since the military coup, escalating violence continued to take an insufferable toll on the civilian population. According to credible sources,1 as at 31 May 2025, the military had killed a minimum of 6,764 civilians over that time period, including 1,409 women and 810 children. The military had arrested 29,209 individuals on political grounds, of whom 22,074 remained in detention, including 4,234 women and 235 children. Military-controlled courts had sentenced 172 detainees to death without respect for due process, fair trial rights or judicial guarantees of independence. (p. 2\)  “Beyond such incidents, civilians, especially ethnic minority communities in areas where pervasive fighting continued, remained exposed to multiple human rights violations, abuses and associated protection risks, including through conscription and forced recruitment, arbitrary deprivation of liberty, sexual violence, the denial of humanitarian assistance, particularly during natural disasters, food insecurity and the spread of communicable diseases due to a lack of services and inadequate living conditions. Also of serious concern was the increase in reports of trafficking in persons in relation to scam centres. Such centres are reportedly enabled by entrenched impunity, rampant corruption and a weakened rule of law since the military’s seizure of power. Further investigation and analysis of the impact on the civilian population of the transnational criminal networks that run the centres are needed.” (p. 6\)  "In northern Rakhine State, the Rohingya continued to suffer many human rights violations and abuses, some of which were reminiscent of the 2017 atrocities. Both the military and the Arakan Army reportedly carried out air and artillery strikes, resulting in the killing of civilians and the destruction of civilian objects, and killings, enforced disappearances, torture and ill-treatment, arbitrary deprivation of liberty and the damage and destruction, looting and occupation of civilian property, in addition to the forced recruitment of Rohingya. While conflict-related violence affecting the Rohingya has decreased in 2025, the Arakan Army has reportedly perpetrated numerous abuses and violations against the Rohingya with impunity. Conduct by Rohingya armed groups, including the Arakan Rohingya Salvation Army, have heightened protection concerns and contributed to widespread fear and further displacement.” (p. 8\)   |
