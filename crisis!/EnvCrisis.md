|  Explanation of Crisis  | The environmental crisis in Haiti is not clear but a multifaceted issue. The environmental and scientific issue  of Haiti’s climate experiences ignite social and humanitarian crises that add complexity of the climate related fight at hand.   Haiti’s environmental crisis has become a deep, compounding emergency shaped by deforestation, weak waste management, climate shocks, and chronic institutional fragility. The country’s ecological decline has exposed citizens to it’s vulnerability to floods, landslides, hurricanes, drought, water stress, and food insecurity, while pollution and uncontrolled urban growth continue to worsen daily living conditions. For decades, Haiti has relied heavily on charcoal and firewood for energy, helping drive severe forest loss and leaving only a tiny share of the country covered by forest. That degradation has stripped hillsides of protection, made watersheds far more vulnerable, and increased the damage caused by heavy rains and tropical storms. At the same time, rapid urbanization has outpaced public services, especially in Port-au-Prince, where solid waste regularly clogs drainage channels and contributes to flooding, air pollution, and public health risks. The combined effects of environmental decline, displacement, poverty, and recurring disasters have made recovery difficult, while climate change is now intensifying sea-level rise, changing rainfall patterns, and extreme weather impacts across the country. In this sense, Haiti’s environmental crisis is not a single disaster but a prolonged struggle in which deforestation, pollution, climate vulnerability, and weak governance reinforce one another, leaving the population exposed to some of the harshest conditions in the Caribbean. The core crisis boils down to one of the most notable instances of climate change directly destroying livelihoods, and an area of the world that climate action and legislation left behind. Paying the price for the world’s pollution, Haitians don’t only struggle from extreme weather, which has plagued them for many years, but environmental changes in the living and agricultural environment which translate to economic destruction, structural unemployment and uncertainty.  Soil has changed in its properties, making it less fertile and instead too easily soluble; combinations of deep poverty perpetuated by climate change drive overfishing creating a vicious cycle for fishing families. Struggles for sustenance contribute to violence in the capital, Port-Au Prince. This century’s follow up to colonial deforestation, soil erosion plagues Haiti’s natural forests, as only 63% of natural forest land recorded in 2002 is estimated to remain. Agricultural destruction and erosion fans local pessimism about Haitian futures. If no use can be made of land, what future is left? Flooding and subsequent droughts strengthen their chokehold on the Haitian working class, and push some individuals into the desperation of violence. As youth struggle with food and climate insecurity, many are swept up by the supposed security of gang affiliation. Many climate migrants find themselves targets of gang violence as they move into larger, but more overcrowded cities where makeshift housing is created. Highly emotional stories of these migrants include Hatian individuals forced to flee from the capital they sought refuge in, back to lands that have been pillaged of natural relevance.  While Haiti is responsible for a negligible amount of global emissions, the global heavyweights share their responsibilities for exacerbating the Haitian climate crisis. More so, colonizing countries like France responsible for parts of Haitian infrastructure are somewhat materially responsible. Systemic poverty leaves even Port-Au-Prince without a central sewage system, exacerbating the effects of heavy rain and flooding. Sandwiched in between effects of tropical storms, earthquakes, erosion, deforestation for charcoal, political instability and insufficient infrastructure, the climate crisis in Haiti combines with material realities to create a dire situation. Basic Facts: Modern Timeline: 2010 \- Present  Place: Haiti  Status Quo: Soil erosion, deforestation and overfishing plague Haitian residents as the consequences of the rest of the world become clear for island nations. These pessimistic conditions push residents into greater desperation in societal conditions of political and food insecurity.  Impacts: Direct impacts on the Haitian economy, attitude, optimism and material existence of sustenance Canada UNDP- Climate Change Adaption Facility (p.1) Haiti is one of the most vulnerable countries to the impacts of climate change, given its acute poverty, rapid population growth and unplanned urbanization. Climate change is already impacting the population in Haiti through increased temperatures, decreased precipitation, more erratic rainfall, sea level rise and increased frequency of climatic hazards. These changes, directly impacting soil conditions, water availability and extreme events, will affect the agriculture sector which employs 66% of the population. As a result, food insecurity will be further exacerbated, when 30% of households are already ‘moderately food insecure’. Women, who represent 43% of the agricultural labour and produce half of the country’s food crops, will bear most of the burden.  UNICEF reporting Tree felling, a leading contributor to climate change in Haiti, has become a means of survival for many families. Given the economic hardships that plague our nation, this is an understandable choice. However, our silence in the face of this environmentally destructive practice is inexcusable. It is imperative to implement educational, community-oriented, and normative initiatives to raise awareness about the imperative to protect our environment. Every citizen bears the responsibility of safeguarding our surroundings to stave off long-term climate-related calamities. Paradoxically, those contributing to the degradation of Haiti's environment are often the first to voice their complaints when droughts and floods ravage their communities.  |
